What Are Cool Roof Requirements

California's cool roof requirements are part of the state's Title 24 Building Energy Efficiency Standards, designed to reduce energy consumption by requiring roofing materials that reflect more sunlight and absorb less heat. For Bay Area homeowners planning a roof replacement, understanding these requirements prevents surprise costs, ensures code compliance, and can actually lower your energy bills. The rules apply differently depending on your roof type, climate zone, and whether you're doing a full replacement or a repair, so knowing the specifics for your situation is essential before you start getting estimates.

SRI and Reflectance Standards

A cool roof is defined by two key measurements: solar reflectance (how much sunlight the surface reflects) and thermal emittance (how efficiently the surface radiates absorbed heat). Together, these determine the Solar Reflectance Index (SRI), which is the primary metric used for compliance. For steep-slope residential roofs (pitch greater than 2:12), California requires a minimum aged solar reflectance of 0.20 and thermal emittance of 0.75, or a minimum SRI of 16. For low-slope commercial roofs, the requirements are more stringent: aged solar reflectance of 0.63 and thermal emittance of 0.75, or SRI of 75. These aged values account for the natural reduction in reflectance that occurs as roofing materials weather over their first three years.

Climate Zones in the Bay Area

California is divided into 16 climate zones, and cool roof requirements vary by zone. Most Bay Area cities fall in Climate Zones 3, 4, or 5. Zone 3 covers coastal areas like San Francisco, Pacifica, and Half Moon Bay—these areas have mild temperatures and cool roof requirements are less impactful on energy savings but still apply to new construction and re-roofing projects. Zone 4 covers inland areas like Oakland, San Jose, and Livermore where summer temperatures are higher and cool roofs provide more significant energy savings. Zone 5 covers parts of the North Bay. The requirements apply to all new construction, additions, and alterations (including re-roofing) across all residential climate zones in California.

Compliant Material Options

Meeting cool roof requirements doesn't mean you're limited to white or light-colored roofing. Modern cool roof technology has expanded the options significantly. GAF offers cool-rated shingles in their Timberline CS (Cool Series) line, available in multiple dark and medium colors that still meet reflectance requirements through advanced granule technology. Owens Corning's Duration Premium Cool line similarly offers darker colors with cool roof compliance. Metal roofing with factory-applied cool coatings meets requirements in virtually any color. For flat roofs, white TPO and PVC membranes naturally exceed cool roof minimums, and even modified bitumen is available with cool granule cap sheets.

Cost Impact Analysis

The cost impact of choosing cool roof compliant materials is often minimal. Cool-rated asphalt shingles typically cost $5 to $15 more per square (100 sq ft) than their standard counterparts—on a typical 2,000 sq ft roof, that's an additional $100 to $300 total. The energy savings from reduced cooling costs typically recoup this premium within two to four years, especially in warmer inland Bay Area cities. For flat roofs, white TPO is already the default choice and meets cool roof requirements without any cost premium. The real cost impact comes if you choose a non-compliant material and need additional insulation upgrades to compensate, which can add several thousand dollars to your project.

Exemptions and Alternatives

Several exemptions and alternatives exist under Title 24. Roofs with less than 50 percent of their area being replaced may qualify for a repair exemption rather than triggering full cool roof compliance. Buildings with high-performance attic insulation (R-25 or greater for steep-slope, R-20 for low-slope) may use the prescriptive compliance path that allows non-cool materials if insulation exceeds minimum requirements. Historical buildings in designated historic districts may qualify for exemptions if cool roof materials would alter the building's historic character. Radiant barriers installed in the attic can also offset cool roof requirements in some cases. Your roofing contractor should evaluate which compliance path makes the most sense for your specific project and budget.

Key takeaways

  • California Title 24 requires minimum solar reflectance and thermal emittance for all re-roofing projects
  • Cool-rated shingles are available in dark colors and cost only $5–$15 more per square
  • Most Bay Area cities are in Climate Zones 3–5 where cool roof requirements apply
  • Exemptions exist for partial replacements, high insulation levels, and historic buildings
