Skylight Repair Vs Replacement

Repair or replace your skylight? Compare costs, common issues, and factors that determine the best option for your roof.

Repair vs Replace Decision

Leaking skylights are a frequent call we receive, especially during Bay Area rainy seasons. The decision to repair or replace depends on the leak source, skylight age, and the overall condition of the unit.

When Repair Is Sufficient

Repair is usually sufficient when: the leak is from deteriorated flashing or sealant (not the skylight itself), the skylight is less than 15 years old, the glass is clear (no fogging between panes), the frame is structurally sound with no cracking or warping, and the operating mechanism works properly (for venting models).

Common Repairs & Costs

Common repairs and costs: Flashing replacement ($300-$800)—the most common repair, especially when done during a re-roof. Sealant renewal ($150-$400)—resealing the frame-to-glass junction and frame-to-curb junction. Glass unit replacement ($400-$1,200)—when the insulated glass has fogged but the frame is sound.

When to Replace

Replacement is recommended when: the skylight is over 20 years old (seal technology and glass coatings have improved dramatically), the frame is cracked, warped, or corroded, multiple components need repair simultaneously (total repair cost approaches replacement cost), you're replacing the roof (this is the ideal time—saves significant labor cost), or you want to upgrade to a venting model for better ventilation.

Modern Skylight Advantages

Modern skylights offer significant advantages over older units: better insulation values (low-E coatings, argon fill), solar-powered venting with rain sensors, impact-resistant glass options, improved flashing systems, and smart home integration.

Our Recommendation

Our typical recommendation: if repair costs exceed 40-50% of replacement cost, or if the skylight is over 15 years old, replacement provides better long-term value. During a roof replacement, we always recommend replacing skylights simultaneously—the labor savings make it the most cost-effective time.

Energy Efficiency Gains from Replacement

Replacing an older skylight with a modern ENERGY STAR-qualified unit delivers measurable energy efficiency gains. Current Velux skylights achieve U-values of 0.28–0.43 compared to 0.70+ on units from the early 2000s—a dramatic improvement in thermal performance. SHGC ratings of 0.21–0.39 give homeowners control over solar heat gain depending on glass selection. A new skylight can reduce electric lighting costs by 30–80% in the room it serves, and California Title 24 requires that replacement skylights meet current energy code standards for fenestration. ENERGY STAR qualification requires a U-factor of 0.55 or lower and SHGC of 0.30 or lower in our climate zone, which most current Velux models exceed.

Drawbacks to Consider

Be aware of potential drawbacks when deciding between repair and replacement. South- and west-facing skylights contribute to summer heat gain—if your existing unit faces these directions, replacement is an opportunity to upgrade to Low-E glass or add integral blinds for heat management. Rain noise on skylight glass can be reduced by selecting laminated glass during replacement. Leaks remain the most common skylight complaint and are overwhelmingly caused by improper installation or deteriorated flashing rather than the skylight unit itself—using a certified installer with manufacturer flashing kits virtually eliminates this risk. UV exposure through older skylights without modern coatings can fade furniture and flooring; replacement units include UV-blocking coatings that filter 95%+ of damaging rays.

Building Code & Permit Requirements

Any skylight replacement must comply with current Bay Area building codes. California Residential Code Section R308.6 requires tempered or laminated safety glass for all skylight glazing—a standard met by all current manufacturer units but not necessarily by older skylights being repaired. Most Bay Area jurisdictions, including San Francisco, Berkeley, Redwood City, and San Jose, require a building permit for skylight replacement, especially when the roof opening is altered. OSHA and Cal/OSHA fall-through protection standards apply during installation on commercial properties. If the skylight is in a bedroom, verify that the installation meets California egress requirements. Title 24 energy compliance is mandatory for replacement skylights and must be documented on the building permit application.

Key takeaways

  • Repair is best for skylights under 15 years with isolated flashing or sealant issues
  • Replace when the skylight is 20+ years old or multiple components need work
  • If repair costs exceed 40–50% of replacement cost, replacing is better value
  • Roof replacement is the ideal time to replace skylights—significant labor savings
  • Replacement skylights achieve U-values of 0.28–0.43 vs 0.70+ on older units
  • California code requires tempered/laminated glass and Title 24 energy compliance
  • South/west-facing skylights benefit from Low-E glass upgrade during replacement
