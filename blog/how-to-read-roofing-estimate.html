Why Estimates Matter

A roofing estimate is more than just a number at the bottom of a page—it's a detailed scope of work that tells you exactly what you're paying for, what materials will be used, and what protections you have if something goes wrong. Understanding how to read and compare estimates is one of the most important steps in any roofing project. Unfortunately, many homeowners choose contractors based on price alone without examining what's actually included. This guide explains what every line item means, what should always be included, and the warning signs that an estimate isn't telling you the full story.

What a Complete Estimate Includes

A thorough roofing estimate should include the following elements: a detailed scope of work describing every step from tear-off to cleanup, specific material brands and product lines (not just 'asphalt shingles' but 'GAF Timberline HDZ Charcoal'), quantities measured in roofing squares (100 sq ft each), underlayment type and coverage area, ice and water shield locations, flashing details for all penetrations and transitions, ventilation specifications (ridge vent linear footage, soffit vent count), drip edge type and material, and disposal method for old roofing material. Any estimate missing these details is incomplete.

Red Flags to Watch For

Several red flags should make you think twice about a roofing contractor's estimate. Vague pricing that lumps everything into a single line item prevents you from understanding what you're actually getting. No mention of permits is a serious concern—most Bay Area jurisdictions require permits for re-roofing, and skipping permits can void your warranty and create problems when selling your home. Missing contractor license number (CSLB number) means you can't verify their credentials. Unusually low pricing often means corners will be cut on materials, labor, or code compliance. Verbal-only estimates with nothing in writing provide zero protection if disputes arise.

Comparing Multiple Bids

When comparing multiple bids, line them up side by side and check each element: Are they using the same material grade? Is one including ice and water shield while another skips it? Does one include ventilation upgrades while another ignores it? Are permit fees included or excluded? Is the warranty manufacturer-backed or contractor-only? Price differences between estimates often come down to differences in scope rather than overcharging. A higher estimate that includes complete underlayment, proper ventilation, and manufacturer-certified installation may deliver far more value than a lower bid that cuts corners on these critical elements.

Understanding Common Line Items

Understanding common line items helps you evaluate whether an estimate is fair. Tear-off and disposal typically costs $1 to $2 per square foot and includes removing old shingles, felt, and damaged decking. Decking replacement runs $3 to $5 per square foot for sections where plywood has rotted or delaminated. Underlayment (synthetic felt) costs $0.50 to $1 per square foot. Ice and water shield in valleys and penetrations adds $1.50 to $3 per square foot for those areas. Ridge vent installation averages $10 to $15 per linear foot. Permit fees in the Bay Area range from $200 to $600 depending on the city. Dumpster and cleanup is typically $300 to $500.

Key takeaways

  • A good estimate lists specific material brands, quantities, and individual line items
  • Red flags include vague pricing, no permit mention, and missing CSLB license number
  • Compare bids by scope and materials, not just total price—cheaper often means less included
  • Always get written estimates with warranty details before signing any contract
