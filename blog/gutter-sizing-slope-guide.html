Why Sizing & Slope Are Different

Gutter sizing and gutter slope answer different questions. Sizing asks how much runoff a gutter-and-downspout system must carry; slope asks whether each installed run falls toward its outlet. Start with each tributary roof design area and the local design rainfall, then check the number and length of gutter sections, outlet layout, downspout geometry, and gutter profile. A nominal gutter size is not a universal capacity rating.

Worked Example: Roof Area + Rainfall

Worked example (illustrative inputs, not a San Francisco rainfall claim): if a drainage section has a 1,000-square-foot design area and the authority having jurisdiction supplies a 2-inch-per-hour design intensity, the runoff input is 2,000 square-foot-inches per hour. Using 0.623 gallons per square foot-inch, that is about 20.8 gallons per minute (2,000 × 0.623 ÷ 60). This is the flow target for that tributary area; it does not select a 5-inch, 6-inch, or any other gutter by itself.

Use Local Design Data

The example follows the approach in SMACNA's Downspout & Gutter Sizing Calculator, which is based on the Architectural Sheet Metal Manual. SMACNA says rainfall intensity may be entered from the local authority having jurisdiction and points to NOAA precipitation-frequency data; its design-area wizard also accounts for roof shape and vertical-wall area under the International Plumbing Code. For a real project, use the applicable local design event rather than borrowing a rainfall number from another city or a weather app.

Downspout and Run Limitations

Downspouts limit the design too. SMACNA identifies the number of sections, section length, number of downspouts, and gutter width-to-height ratio as inputs; its calculator also notes that 50 feet is a practical maximum gutter length served by one downspout to limit thermal expansion. That is a design check, not a universal flow capacity or permission to run every roof through one outlet. Valleys, adjoining roof planes, wall runoff, debris screens, and partial blockage can increase or restrict the water reaching a section.

Slope & Outlet Layout

Slope should be laid out from the high point of each run to its outlet using the gutter manufacturer's installation instructions and the applicable code or project requirements. Check the actual fall after fascia repairs and hanger installation, and divide long or complex runs when the layout calls for another outlet. Do not treat a single published pitch as a guarantee: slope, profile, outlet size, rainfall, tributary area, and run length work together.

Installation Checks & Warning Signs

Choose gutter material and connection details for the building, exposure, movement, corrosion compatibility, and fascia condition. Manufacturer instructions should control joining, hangers, outlets, end caps, and overflow provisions. Signs that the design or installation needs review include overflow below an otherwise clear edge, standing water after rain, sagging or reverse fall, leaks at seams or outlets, splash marks, and downspouts that discharge too close to the building. A gutter contractor can check the drainage layout; Roof Express offers gutter service.

Key Takeaways

  • Sizing starts with each tributary roof area and local design rainfall; slope separately establishes fall toward an outlet
  • A 1,000-square-foot area at an illustrative 2-inch-per-hour intensity produces about 20.8 gallons per minute—not a universal gutter-size recommendation
  • SMACNA's method also considers section length, outlet count, downspouts, profile dimensions, and local rainfall data
  • Downspout and overflow checks must account for roof shape, wall runoff, debris, screens, and partial blockage
  • Use the gutter service page for installation or drainage-layout help; have long or complex runs evaluated on site
